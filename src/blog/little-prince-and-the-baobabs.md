Grown-ups love figures. — It is the sharpest cut in *The Little Prince*.

Figures make us feel we have the world in our hands: age, weight, income, rank, follower count. But the Little Prince would ask: a flower, a star, a sheep — what figure do you measure them by?

This is a reading note on *The Little Prince*, from chapter four to chapter seven, and through it all I see one line running: **how the grown-ups lose the world — and how we might keep our own.**

## The Sickness: How the Grown-Ups Lose the World

Chapter four gives three scenes that are hard to laugh at.

The Turkish astronomer discovered asteroid B-612 and reported it at a congress in his traditional costume — nobody believed him. In a European suit, the same report was accepted without question. For grown-ups, the standard of truth was never fact or evidence, but **who is speaking, and in what clothes.**

When grown-ups describe a new friend, they don't ask about his voice, the games he plays, the butterflies he collects. They ask: how old? How many brothers? How much does he weigh? How much does his father earn? A living person is flattened into a row of comparable numbers. Numbers become the only currency for measuring value — understanding between people breaks, and loneliness begins.

And then the saddest line of all: "Perhaps I am a little like the grown-ups. I have had to grow old." It is not a sigh about age. What aging means here is **losing the ability to see the sheep inside the box** — the loss of imagination and of the power to believe, and knowing it, clearly, about yourself.

## The Cause: Every Baobab Begins as a Seedling You Were Too Lazy to Pull

Chapter five hands over the "cause" on the diagnosis sheet.

On the Little Prince's planet, two kinds of seeds grow: useful radishes and roses — and baobabs. The trouble is that good and bad are too small to tell apart at first. A baobab is not a small flaw; it stands for whatever can **systematically destroy a life**: some addiction, a draining relationship, an obsession.

It destroys in three steps: first it takes over all the space, until you have no room left for what is good; then its roots reach through your foundations and shake your core values; finally it bursts the whole planet apart. The most dangerous thing is never the sudden blow — it is **the daily neglect that looks harmless at first and takes everything back later.**

So the Little Prince gives himself a rule of iron: **the moment you recognize one, pull it out at once.** And force yourself, every day, to do the weeding — a dull ritual, and the only preservative for the inner world. Keeping your inner world is never a one-time feat; it is a small daily insistence.

The baobabs have changed their names today: the short-video app you open for "just a minute"; the relationship you "put up with for now"; the procrastination you keep putting off as "later." They all appear harmless, and quietly grow over the whole planet.

## Loneliness: Forty-Three Sunsets

Chapter six is the lightest and heaviest chapter in the book.

The Little Prince says his planet is so small that to see a sunset he only had to move his chair a few steps. He had seen forty-three sunsets. He said it in the perfect tense — he had really seen them all. And after seeing them, he fell silent.

Why the silence? Because the sadness was too heavy for language to carry; and deeper still, he realized: **the man sitting in front of him could never truly understand him, his rose, or his volcanoes.** No matter how beautiful a sunset is, if no one truly understands why you love watching it — beauty itself becomes a burden.

This is the feeling of loneliness. It is different from the sickness and the cause: even when you clearly know where the problem is, the loneliness cannot be dissolved. Some beauty is fated to be watched alone.

## The Root: Mushrooms and "Matters of Consequence"

Chapter seven is where the root of loneliness finally shows itself.

"I" was busy fixing the engine — a "matter of consequence" — and brushed off the Little Prince's painful question about his flower. The Little Prince did not get angry. He only said: "You talk just like the grown-ups. You mix everything up together."

It is one of the harshest rebukes in the book. And the sharper metaphor comes right after: the grown-ups who repeat all day "I am a serious man" are, in the author's pen — **not men, but mushrooms**. No roots, no leaves, no flowers: no individuality, no vitality, no curiosity, love, or sorrow left — only a hollow self-inflation.

"Matters of consequence" become the most respectable way to brush someone off, and the deepest wound to the heart is exactly this kind of brushing off. Busy, serious, earnest — three words that sound entirely correct can kill every question inside a person.

## Keeping: Don't Let the Inner Questioner Fall Silent

Put the four chapters together and one complete line appears: the grown-ups are sick (chapter four) — the cause is letting the baobabs grow (chapter five) — in the sick world nobody understands beauty anymore (chapter six) — because mushrooms are everywhere (chapter seven).

What the Little Prince is really asking is not how bad the grown-up world is, but: **in a world measured by figures and "matters of consequence," how do you keep your rose?**

The answer the book gives is almost embarrassingly plain: like pulling baobabs, bend down once every day. Keep your curiosity, keep the impulse to ask "why," keep the self that falls silent for the sheep inside the box.

When I finished chapter seven and wrote these notes, I suddenly understood: the fact that I can write this analysis means my own Little Prince is still here — and wide awake. I will keep recognizing those hollow, alienated voices — and keep my rose and my volcanoes safe.
